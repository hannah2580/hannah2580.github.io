Policies to address the effects of Malaysia's Ageing Population

Sep 2025 · Essay Competition

1 Introduction

An ageing society is one where 7% or more of its population is aged 65 and above, and an aged society when more than 14% are aged 65 and above (United Nations). Malaysia, an upper-middle-income nation, is projected to become an aged nation by 2040. Individuals aged over 60 comprised 11.6% of the population in 2024 and are expected to reach 17% by 2040 due to longer life expectancies and a declining fertility rate (DOSM). This has many implications for Malaysia’s workforce, economic growth, and healthcare costs. Hence, this essay discusses policies Malaysia should implement to curb these effects.

2 Effects of an Ageing Population in Malaysia

2.1 Retirement Inadequacy

Malaysia’s pension system only uses the pay-as-you-go (PAYG) model for public sector workers, who account for 10% of Malaysia’s labour force (Holzmann, 2015). The majority of workers, particularly the 52% in the private sector, rely on a defined contribution mandatory savings scheme known as the Employment Provident Fund (EPF). By requiring individuals to fund their own retirement, this system mitigates the pressure on Malaysia’s pension system that would otherwise result from a shrinking ratio of working-age taxpayers to retirees in an ageing population. However, the EPF system does not guarantee retirement adequacy: over half of EPF members at age 55 fail to meet the basic savings threshold for essential retirement expenses, with many expected to exhaust their savings within five years (KWSP, 2024). This shortfall is primarily driven by the low wage structure of the Malaysian labour market and low levels of financial literacy, which limit savings for retirement (Suhaimi, 2024). Informal workers, representing 38% of the labour force, face even greater vulnerability as they are excluded from the EPF system entirely. Retirement adequacy is further diminished due to the poor coverage and benefits of social assistance pensions, particularly the Bantuan Warga Emas (BWE). The BWE only covers about 4.2% of the elderly in hardcore poverty. However, nearly one-third of Malaysian seniors live in households below the national poverty line but are ineligible for support, leaving them vulnerable. As Malaysia’s ageing population rapidly grows, policies must ensure retirees are financially prepared, especially informal and low-income workers.

2.2 Strained Healthcare System

Demand for healthcare rises with age due to the higher prevalence of chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs) among older adults (Haron et al, 2024). This has created excess demand in heavily subsidised public healthcare, leading to long waiting times and overcrowding (Isa et al, 2022). Without effective policies, an ageing population will further strain the system. The ageing population has also driven up the demand for long-term care to help with daily activities, especially for those with chronic conditions such as dementia. Institutional care struggles to meet the increased care needs due to shortages of trained caregivers and geriatric specialists.

2.3 Labour Market

Malaysia’s ageing population is set to significantly impact the labour market, as older individuals are much less likely to work compared to younger cohorts. Labour force participation was only 39.2% for those aged 60-64 compared with 68.4% for the overall population. Employment among individuals aged 55-65 was 45.2%, significantly lower compared to other ageing countries (World Bank, 2020). The shortfall is expected to contribute to a third of the decline in GDP growth by 2050 (World Bank, 2020). Lower labour market participation with older age primarily stems from a lack of suitable employment opportunities and skill gaps. Consequently, many older workers earn low incomes or remain in informal jobs, contributing to higher rates of old-age poverty (Bernama, 2023).

3 Current Policies and Recommendations

3.1 Addressing Retirement Inadequacy

Improving retirement adequacy among EPF members requires addressing low wages and financial literacy. Higher wages increase an individual’s ability to save or contribute to the EPF, while people with higher financial literacy levels are more likely to save and be prepared for retirement than those with low financial literacy (Zulfaka et al, 2020).

Informal and low-income workers are particularly vulnerable to low wages, which constrain their ability to save for retirement. Policymakers should prioritise supporting this group through improved savings mechanisms and expanding social protection. One initiative, such as the i-Saaran scheme, allows informal workers to make voluntary contributions to the EPF, complemented by a 20% government matching incentive and the standard annual EPF dividend (KWSP, 2024). However, the voluntary enrolment has resulted in limited uptake; i-Saraan only covered about 15% of Malaysia’s informal workforce (Bernama, 2025) due to a lack of awareness about the program or low financial literacy to recognise its benefits. To enhance coverage, introducing an auto-enrollment mechanism for informal workers would ensure consistent savings behaviour by overcoming behavioural barriers such as inertia and short-termism (Madrian et al, 2001). This reform can be implemented in phases to give low-income workers and employers time to adjust, coupled with continued governmental matching incentives and targeted tax relief to ease compliance. Additionally, expanding the Bantuan Warga Emas (BWE) scheme to cover a larger share of low-income seniors would provide a critical safety net for those who remain unable to accumulate sufficient savings. Evidence from South Korea and Mexico demonstrates that expanding non-contributory social pensions substantially reduces old-age poverty (Clemente et al, 2017). Given that BWE currently accounts for only 0.038% of GDP, compared to the estimated 1.5% for a universal pension (KRI, 2023), there is fiscal capacity for gradual expansion. These reforms would strengthen the retirement security of low-wage and informal workers, addressing the savings constraints imposed by low wages.

To tackle low financial literacy, the government has implemented several nationwide and statewide financial education initiatives, such as the ongoing “My Money & Me” program. However, these programs are often one-off, informational workshops or short-term courses, which produce short-term improvements in financial knowledge but have limited impact on long-term financial behaviour (Bank Negara Malaysia, 2024). To improve effectiveness, financial education should be embedded across the life course, such as in schools and workplace programs. More importantly, these programs must enable practical applications. For instance, schools could incorporate experiential learning activities, such as budgeting simulations or project-based activities, rather than relying solely on informational lessons. Such approaches are more likely to promote long-term savings habits and ultimately improve retirement preparedness.

3.2 Addressing the Strained Healthcare System

To ease excess demand for public healthcare, policymakers should shift from curative care to preventive care. Chronic diseases are largely preventable with regular health screenings and healthy lifestyles, which are far less costly than treatment (Ismail, 2025). To incentivise regular health screenings, the government could collaborate with private insurers to offer reduced premiums for individuals who maintain good health, verified through Malaysia’s MySejahtera digital health platform. Nutrition is another essential preventive measure, as poor nutrition is a key driver of Malaysia’s rising rates of NCDs (Goh et al, 2020). While the sugar tax aims to curb diet-related NCDs, its effectiveness has been limited by the low price elasticity of demand for unhealthy foods and the continued accessibility and convenience of such options. Policymakers should therefore complement this measure with targeted subsidies for local fruit and vegetable producers to make healthy foods cheaper and widely available. Promoting health screenings and improved nutrition would ensure adults age healthily and reduce unnecessary hospitalisations or advanced treatments, thus alleviating the strain on public healthcare services.

To address the shortage of trained caregivers, policymakers should restructure Malaysia’s existing capacity-building program for community health volunteers (CHVs) to expand its focus beyond NCD prevention and include long-term eldercare. The program demonstrated that CHVs can effectively deliver community-based health interventions and positively influence the health behaviours of local populations (Ahmad Fahmy et al, 2022), demonstrating their potential to undertake larger responsibilities. By adapting the program curriculum to include modules on geriatric care, CHVs could help address growing long-term care needs and alleviate pressure on public healthcare.

3.3 Addressing Labour Market Challenges

Malaysia already has several measures to address the low labour force participation (LFP) of seniors, such as raising the mandatory retirement age to 65 and expanding reskilling initiatives (United Nations, 2022). However, these interventions often overlook socioeconomic differences, which may limit their effectiveness in improving LFP for certain groups.

To improve LFP among low-educated, low-income seniors, reskilling programs such as the Senior Back in Action (SEBA) program must be reformed. SEBA provides skill development training to help seniors secure productive employment opportunities (HRD Corp, 2025). However, it has limited seats and requires certain resources and digital literacy to attend the training, making it rivalrous and excludable. Thus, low-income seniors with fewer resources are less likely to participate. To address this, quotas could prioritise training slots for seniors with at most primary education, and additional support such as travel subsidies could broaden access for seniors in rural areas, where low-socioeconomic-status seniors are concentrated (Shahar et al, 2019). Linking training to job placement pipelines in high-demand sectors, such as healthcare and tourism, would further ensure participants aren’t left vulnerable to returning to informal work. To retain higher-educated and high-skilled seniors in the labour force, jobs must cater to their need for greater flexible work arrangements. Flexible work is especially crucial for retaining older women in the labour force, who also have to shoulder domestic and caregiving responsibilities (Subramaniam et al, 2022). Many multinational corporations offer flexible working arrangements for their employees in response to the pandemic, but many small to medium enterprises (SMEs) are still struggling to transition to remote work (Mercer, 2023). The government could therefore support SMEs in implementing age-friendly policies through consultation services or subsidies, as already seen in Japan (Alwin, 2025).
